# Does a pathway optimiser find what clinicians found? A plausibility check against a published Norwegian lung cancer pathway redesign

## Abstract

Before a patient with suspected lung cancer can start treatment, they pass through scans, tissue sampling and specialist meetings, and the order of these steps, the care pathway, largely decides how long they wait. Our simulator represents a hospital's pathway as a flowchart, sends thousands of virtual patients through it and counts how many start treatment within a target time; any change is tested by editing the flowchart and running it again. Our optimiser repeatedly tries small edits to the flowchart in the simulator and keeps those that shorten the wait. To judge whether its suggestions are sensible, we used a published redesign as a reference case, not as a ground truth. Stokstad et al. (2019) found that 40% of 100 patients at one Norwegian hospital started treatment within 42 days, and by recalculating their timelines estimated that a faster PET-CT scanner would raise this to 48% and a reordered pathway to 80%. We rebuilt the hospital's pathway from the paper and fitted five unreported quantities so that our virtual hospital also treated about 40% in time; 184 configurations fitted equally well. The optimiser improved the pathway in every configuration, to 48–77% (median 65%) without new equipment and to 78–93% (median 88%) with the faster scanner, against the paper's 80% (95% confidence interval 72–87%). Its pathway contained the authors' three timing changes plus a fourth that needs clinical review, and performed about the same as the authors' pathway, not better. Running the scanner change in our simulator showed where our virtual hospital differs from the real one: it gives the scanner a median gain of 20 points where the paper estimated 8. The test supports the direction of the optimiser's changes, not the size of its predictions.

## Introduction

A simulator that reports an improvement inside its own model proves little about real care. Its suggestions become worth discussing only if, given a real hospital's pathway, it proposes the kind of changes experienced clinicians would propose and predicts benefits of a plausible size.

Stokstad et al. (2019) offer a rare opportunity to check this. They followed 100 patients with stage I–II lung cancer at St Olavs Hospital in Trondheim (diagnosed 2011–2013) and found that 40% (95% CI 31–50%) started treatment within the national 42-day target, with a median of 46.5 days. They then re-sequenced the recorded intervals to estimate two alternatives. With PET-CT, the staging scan, available within 7 days, 48% (39–58%) would have been treated in time, a difference they found not significant. With their optimised pathway, in which PET-CT and the exercise test are ordered at the first consultation, the tumour board meets straight after them and picks one sampling method for diagnosis and staging, and treatment is booked without waiting for the sample, 80% (72–87%) would have been. The paper does not spell out the patient-level calculation; it uses the observed intervals with the shorter PET-CT wait, and a variant using median intervals gave 93%. Both figures are estimates, not outcomes of a redesign that was carried out.

We therefore do not try to reproduce the paper. Our patients are synthetic, not theirs, and several of the paper's quantities are unreported. Instead we give our flowchart the hospital's structure, normalise it to the same 40% starting point, let the optimiser work on it, and ask five questions. Does it find an improvement? Is the improvement of similar size to the paper's? Is our spread across equally well-fitting configurations comparable to the paper's estimate and interval? Do the proposed pathways contain the authors' structure? And what does running the authors' own changes in our simulator say about how our patients and parameters differ from theirs?

## Methods

The flowchart has a CT scan, a first hospital visit, then tissue sampling, PET-CT and, for patients with reduced lung function, an exercise test, followed by a weekly tumour board, a wait for surgery or radiotherapy, and treatment. Waits are drawn from the intervals the paper reports, and the practice variation it counts is kept, such as the 27 patients whose PET-CT was ordered late and the 15 who needed a repeat bronchoscopy. The flowchart is our reconstruction from the paper's text and counts; its figures were not inspected, and a later check against the text found two differences, described in the Results. All virtual patients have cancer, as in the paper.

Five quantities the simulation needs are not reported: the wait for bronchoscopy, the cutoff for the weekly tumour board, an extra wait when the CT report is not ready at the first visit, the share of on-time PET-CT orders made at referral triage rather than at the first visit, and how much of the referral-to-treatment interval falls after the tumour board. We searched 1,920 combinations of these and kept every one under which the simulated observed pathway gave 40% treated within 42 days (±4 points) and a median of 46.5 days (±2.5 days). This is the normalisation step. We call the accepted combinations fitting configurations and report every result across all of them, with the same 1,000 virtual patients for every flowchart within a configuration.

In each configuration we ran flowcharts that played no part in the fitting: the faster scanner alone, the authors' pathway as we reconstructed it, and the optimiser's pathways. The optimiser is a greedy search. At each step it lists edits of three kinds (stop waiting for a step, start a step earlier, always take one option of a choice), simulates the most promising, and accepts the one that most raises the share treated in time, provided it helps in each of two training cohorts of 1,000 patients and adds no more than 2% to the tests per patient. It was not told the authors' changes, though each can be written as one such edit. It ran at the best-fitting configuration from two starts, the observed pathway and the pathway with the faster scanner, simulating 69 candidate edits each time, and was checked on three cohorts it never saw. The two pathways it found were then evaluated unchanged in all configurations. We also removed each of its changes in turn and ran two variants of the authors' pathway that correct our reconstruction.

## Results

The fitting accepted 184 combinations, giving 36–44% treated in time on the observed pathway. They fit equally well because the two targets carry nearly the same information (a higher share in time goes with a shorter median), so the data pin down a region of the five unknowns, not a point. The optimiser found an improvement in every configuration: 10.6 to 36.0 points (median 24.9) without new equipment. With the faster scanner, which the authors' pathway also assumes, it reached 78–93%, a gain of 38 to 55 points against the paper's 40. The size is of the same order as the authors' estimate but tends to sit above it: it falls inside the published 72–87% in 75 of the 184 configurations and above it in the other 109.

Our range and the published interval measure different things. The paper's interval reflects having 100 patients; ours reflects the unknown quantities, for a fixed proposed pathway. They are of similar width: 100-patient cohorts drawn at our best fit give 72–87% for the authors' pathway and 81–94% for the optimiser's, while across configurations the authors' pathway spans 62–86%. Any single number from this simulator carries both uncertainties.

The optimiser's pathway contains the authors' structure. Three of its four changes are the authors' timing changes: the tumour board no longer waits for sampling to finish, PET-CT is always ordered on time, and the exercise test is always ordered early. Removing any of them lowered the result in every configuration; the PET-CT change mattered most (13.2 points at the median without the scanner). The fourth, planning tissue sampling at referral triage, is not in the paper, is worth 2 to 4 points, overlaps with the first, and needs clinical review because no specialist has yet seen the patient. Two things are missing. The optimiser cannot choose one sampling method at the board, so it keeps the observed number of sampling procedures, while the authors' pathway cuts them to 82% of observed in both the paper and our simulation. And because it ran only at the best fit, we have one proposed pathway per start, not a family of pathways across configurations.

The optimiser appears to beat our reconstruction of the authors' pathway in all 184 configurations, but this is an artefact: in our version treatment waits for a sample requested only after the board, whereas the paper samples while the patient waits for treatment. With that wait removed, the authors' pathway is 0.2 to 2.5 points ahead of the optimiser's in every configuration. The optimiser arrived at the authors' redesign; it did not improve on it. Without new equipment it beats the faster scanner alone in only 128 of 184 configurations.

Running the authors' changes in our simulator shows where our hospital differs from theirs, but only partly. For the full pathway the comparison is confounded by our reconstruction: besides the wait for the sample, our fitted configurations order PET-CT at triage where the paper orders it at the first consultation. Correcting that alone lowers our authors' pathway from 80.1% to 66.1% at the best fit; removing the wait raises it to 88.3%. Each difference exceeds the gap to the paper's 80%, so that gap says little about our patients or parameters. Correcting both gives a median of 79.0% (64.2–89.5%), with 139 configurations inside the published interval instead of 118, though the observed pathway was not refitted under that reading. The scanner change is the cleaner probe, and there we depart clearly: a gain of 13 to 33 points against the paper's 8, with about a third of configurations inside its interval. In our virtual hospital PET-CT lies on the critical path for most patients; in the real cohort other delays, such as the 39 patients whose CT report was not ready at the first visit, evidently held patients back as well. Adding re-discussion of incomplete cases at the board, or correlated delays within a patient, did not close this gap.

## Conclusion

As a plausibility check the result is encouraging on direction and cautious on size. From the hospital's structure at the same 40% starting point, the optimiser found the authors' three timing changes without being told them, its gains were of the same order, and every configuration ranked the authors' pathway above the scanner alone and the scanner above today's pathway, as the paper did. It did not improve on the authors and missed the part of their redesign that reduces sampling. Because our virtual hospital overstates what a faster scanner alone achieves, the optimiser's emphasis on PET-CT, and any claim that reordering beats buying equipment, should be read with that bias in mind.

This kind of simulation is a reasonable way to find reorderings that deserve clinical attention: it can be fitted to the few figures a hospital publishes and tests every proposal on identical virtual patients. It cannot establish how much a redesign will help. Two published figures form one constraint on five unknowns, the reference figures are themselves estimates from one hospital and 100 patients who all had cancer, and our flowchart is a reconstruction. A real validation would need patient-level timelines to fit the unknowns directly and a prospective before-and-after comparison of an implemented change. For another hospital the procedure is the same: rebuild its flowchart, fit the unreported waits to its observed share and median, report results across all fitting configurations, and hand the proposed changes to clinicians as candidates to review, not as forecasts.
