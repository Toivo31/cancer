# Q&A prep: the 20 hardest questions

Short answers, about 15 to 30 seconds each. Numbers are filled from the bundle and the robustness files (`filled/qa.md`); the single place to update a number is `numbers_sheet.md`. Questions 1 to 12 are the original set (wording corrected after the robustness check); 13 to 20 are the added hard ones. Where an answer depends on a pending team decision (D17, D18, D20) it says so.

**1. Isn't this just synthetic?**
Yes, all of it, and every results screen says so. Two things stand in for real data: every scenario input carries a source tag or is marked as an assumption, and the engine is checked against a published Norwegian pathway (the check gives a band, not a point; question 13). Be plain about the split: the Finnish scenario file has [[f_scn_n]] input blocks (`docs/ledger.md`), [[f_scn_sourced]] sourced and [[f_scn_assumption]] assumptions, and the assumptions include every capacity and booking lead (calibrated to published waits); the Norwegian file has [[s_scn_n]] input blocks, [[s_scn_assumption]] assumptions. The larger parameter table is evidence and calibration targets, not all simulator inputs. The claim is "worth piloting", not "works in Finland".

**2. Why should Finland trust a Norwegian calibration?**
It does not have to. The Norwegian case tests the engine's reordering logic against one published result, with only the observed pathway fitted. Finnish numbers come from a separate scenario with Finnish sources and assumed capacities. One study, one centre, 100 patients, specialist care only.

**3. Does it match the Norwegian paper?**
It is consistent with it, not an exact match. Fitted: [[s_obs_share]]% against [[s_pub_base]]%, the only thing we fitted. Predicted: for the authors' pathway we get [[s_auth_share]]% at our best fit against their [[s_pub_opt]]%, but across all [[s_ext_n]] settings that fit the observed data the range is [[s_ext_auth_min]] to [[s_ext_auth_max]]%, typically about [[s_ext_auth_med]]%. We miss on the faster scanner alone: [[s_fast_share]]% against [[s_pub_fast]]%. What holds everywhere is the ranking. The authors' 80% is itself a model estimate. Do not say "we predicted 80% exactly".

**4. How does it avoid hindsight bias?**
Proposals are scored on groups from revealed timestamps only, not by judging a late test on patients who failed earlier ones. Acceptance is a rerun of the same patients (common random numbers), and we report on held-out patients. Selection optimism: [[s_opt_optimism_pp]] points (Norwegian) and [[f_optimism_pp]] (Finnish; negative means the unseen patients did slightly better). Did the Norwegian search "find" the authors' answer blind? See question 15.

**5. The acceptance step uses simulated truth. Isn't that cheating?**
It is the one step with no real-world equivalent, and we say so. In practice it is a monitored pilot. Proposals never see the hidden condition.

**6. Where is the AI?**
Three places: the optimiser (search over flowchart changes), the decision model (a transformer-style model that proposes the next step and is benchmarked against a dot-product baseline), and the AI-assisted build. The team's fine-tuned decision model (JEV) is connected to the decision nodes, but the runs we show use rules and placeholder decision tables (question 19). Our own small benchmark model, in our in-loop test, matched the hand-written rule rather than beating it, and it showed that a model optimising each patient separately overloads the shared queue unless it can see queue state. We would rather say that than overclaim.

**7. Does the decision model work?**
This is our own small benchmark model, not the team's JEV. On a task where the right answer depends on the option set, it scores [[dm_acc_corr]]% against [[dm_acc_dot_wide]]% for a parameter-matched dot-product baseline ([[dm_acc_dot]]% for the smaller d=32 one). On the control task it also wins by [[dm_ctrl_gain_pp]] points, so part of the gain is capacity. The "half the compute for a second question" claim did not reproduce: [[dm_second_lo]]-[[dm_second_hi]]% at our sizes. Task and rule are ours; no public benchmark yet.

**8. What would a hospital do on Monday?**
Pick one lung unit. Reserve the treatment slot when the case is sent to the tumour board and book the specialist visit when the referral arrives, and measure days from first contact to treatment by step, including booking leads. The simulator says these two reorders take our county from [[f_share_base]]% to [[f_share_opt]]% within [[f_target]] days with no new equipment. A bigger hand-written order-together pathway reaches [[f_bundle_share]]% against [[f_capctl_share]]% for the same extra capacity with today's order, but it needs PET-CT capacity, so check the scanner first.

**9. Why a [[f_target]]-day target?**
Provisional (D20). From first contact, every flowchart is at 0% within the Norwegian 42 days, and we found no Finnish first-contact target. Median days ([[f_median_base]] to [[f_median_opt]]) does not depend on the choice.

**10. Does the change widen inequality?**
Under our assumptions both groups gain ([[f_ns_share_base]]% to [[f_ns_share_opt]]% vague, [[f_ss_share_base]]% to [[f_ss_share_opt]]% alarm). The gap in points [[f_gap_pp_widens]] ([[equity_gap_base_pp]] to [[equity_gap_opt_pp]]); the gap in median days [[f_gap_days_narrows]] ([[f_gap_days_base]] to [[f_gap_days_opt]]). The group difference is our assumption (D9), so this shows how a change reaches each group, not how unequal Finland is.

**11. Does speed come at the cost of missed cancers or other patients?**
Not in the model: missed cancers [[f_missed_base]] to [[f_missed_opt]] per cohort, tests per patient [[f_tests_base]] to [[f_tests_opt]], non-cancer patients leave in [[f_noncancer_base]] to [[f_noncancer_opt]] days. Guardrails include tissue-confirmed diagnoses, added after the optimiser tried skipping sampling; bronchoscopy and needle biopsy are marked as confirming in the Finnish scenario too, so that guardrail is active there. Crowd-out would depend on how many slots a priority track reserves; our accepted changes use none. (In the Norwegian check every patient has cancer, so the missed-cancer guardrail is vacuous there.)

**12. Isn't adding capacity simpler?**
Sometimes. One more slot at the busiest scanner (the [[f_cap_top_name]]) adds [[f_cap_top_pp]] points; the next best adds [[f_cap_second_pp]] and the rest at most [[f_cap_rest_max_pp]]. Hand-written reordering with extra capacity adds [[f_bundle_gain_pp]] points, and the optimiser's two reorders with no new capacity add [[f_share_gain_pp]] (against [[f_capctl_gain_pp]] for a large capacity increase with today's order). The two are not exclusive; the bigger reorder needs the capacity at the scanner it moves work to. Capacity vs order in our county is a result of our assumptions; the Norwegian study is the independent evidence.

## Added: validity, optimiser, data and deployment

**13. Why does the Norway validation say 62 to 86% and not 80%?**
Because the 80% is one point in a band. We fitted two numbers (the share within 42 days and the median) to set five inputs the paper does not report. Those two numbers carry nearly the same information, so many settings fit equally well. We first searched a narrow grid, where the best fit gave 80% and the range was 78 to 86%. When we widened the grid to [[s_ext_grid]] points, [[s_ext_n]] settings fit the observed pathway and the authors' pathway ranged from [[s_ext_auth_min]] to [[s_ext_auth_max]]%, typically about [[s_ext_auth_med]]%. About [[s_ext_auth_in_ci_pct]]% of settings fall inside the paper's own interval ([[val_pub_opt_ci]]%). So: consistent with the paper, ranking confirmed, exact size not pinned down. We found this ourselves and changed the headline. Source: `docs/validation.md`, "Robustness".

**14. You missed the faster-scanner prediction. Why trust the rest?**
We report the miss on the first slide that shows the check. At the best fit we give [[s_fast_share]]% against the published [[s_pub_fast]]% (which was not statistically significant); across settings [[s_ext_fast_min]] to [[s_ext_fast_max]]%, and only [[s_ext_fast_in_ci_pct]]% of settings fall inside the published interval. We tried two fixes (re-discussion at the tumour board; delays that are correlated within a patient) and neither worked. Our reading, not proven: the model puts PET-CT on the critical path of more patients than the real hospital did. That means we probably overstate the value of speeding up one step, so the "order beats capacity" conclusion errs on the cautious side. We do not trust the magnitudes to better than about plus or minus 12 points; we trust the ranking, which holds in all [[s_ext_rank_n]] settings.

**15. Was the optimiser really blind?**
Blind to the hidden diagnosis: yes. Its proposals read only what steps revealed, and a test that scrambles the hidden fields after a run leaves its proposals unchanged. Blind to the answer: no. It chose from a menu of about [[s_candidates]] single changes that we wrote, and the menu contained each of the authors' changes as one edit. It was not told which ones the authors made, and it picked those three plus a fourth. So it shows that, given reasonable building blocks, the search finds the authors' reordering and a further step; it does not show discovery from nothing. A check on how the optimiser's changes hold across the [[s_ext_n]] settings and without the fourth change is running and is not yet claimed.

**16. What is the fourth change, and is it safe?**
It plans tissue sampling when the referral is triaged, before a specialist has seen the patient. It was not in the paper. It is where the optimiser's lead over the authors comes from: [[s_opt_share]]% against the authors' 80%, both with the faster scanner. We have not had it reviewed by a clinician and we do not call it safe. Whether sampling before a specialist visit is clinically appropriate depends on the case (for example the choice of procedure depends on tumour location). It is the first thing to put in front of a clinician; the guardrails the optimiser applies (no fewer tissue-confirmed diagnoses, no extra tests beyond a small tolerance) are met in the simulation (the missed-cancer check is vacuous there, because every Norwegian patient has cancer), which is not the same as clinical approval. Without new equipment the search reaches [[s_noeq_share]]%.

**17. The patients are synthetic. Why would a model trained on them work on real patients?**
We do not know that it would, and we do not claim it. What we can say: the cards follow a catalogue of 11 conditions, 42 hidden variables and 26 tests, and an audit of 38 checks passes. But 177 of about 260 numbers in the catalogue are our assumptions, no clinician has read it, test results are independent of each other given the condition, disease does not progress while waiting, and only chest-symptom presenters are covered. A model trained here can be right about our synthetic world and wrong about real patients. What the synthetic data is good for: building and testing the machinery (the information boundary, the loop, the training export) before real data exists, and checking that a model does not just learn our template wording (train on template cards, test on model-written cards; this tests wording, not validity). The next step is clinician review and then real, permitted records.

**18. What can a decision-maker see, and what is hidden?**
It sees the visible record: background (age, sex, smoking, region), what the patient reported, and the result of each test that has been done, as positive or negative. It never sees the hidden condition, the hidden findings, which results are planned misses or false alarms, or the card's identity. A result appears only when that test is ordered. The record has two layers: a mechanical log written by the engine, and clinical assessments written by the decider. The training export keeps the hidden labels outside the input. This is by construction in the code and covered by tests; we do not claim it is proven leak-free, and an end-to-end review of the boundary is the thing to ask for next.

**19. Are the decisions in your runs made by the JEV?**
No. The runs we show use a rule policy and placeholder decision tables, and every behaviour number in them is an assumption. The team's fine-tuned model (JEV) is connected through an adapter and a step-through tool, so you can walk a patient and see what it proposes. Its scores are relative to the offered options, not disease probabilities. Nothing we report depends on it. Also, our primary-care loop with placeholder tables does not yet reproduce the calibrated Finnish chain (it fails [[e_checks_failed]] of [[e_checks_total]] consistency checks), so we call it a mechanism, not a result.

**20. What would it take to use this in a Finnish wellbeing services county?**
Real data and clinical ownership, in this order. (1) The county's own pathway timestamps (referral, first visit, imaging, tumour board, treatment) to replace our assumed capacities and booking times; these are the assumptions the Finnish numbers lean on most. (2) A permit and data-protection route for using the records (secondary use of health data in Finland goes through Findata; we have not started this). (3) Calibrate on one period and test on another before showing any number. (4) A clinician review of the catalogue and of every proposed change, the fourth Norwegian one first. (5) A monitored pilot in one county with before-and-after measurement. (6) A check of whether any individual-patient use counts as medical-device software; we have not assessed this. Until then our claim is "worth piloting", not "works in Finland".

## If pressed

- The Norwegian optimiser's fourth change plans tissue sampling at referral triage, before a specialist has seen the patient. It is beyond the paper and needs clinical review (D18). The headline with the faster scanner is [[s_opt_share]]%; without new equipment [[s_noeq_share]]% (D17).
- GP-first instead of nurse-first was not accepted: it did not pass our safety checks on the training patients (half a missed cancer per cohort more, one patient in one cohort), while on unseen patients it looked neutral to positive. That is noise-level either way, so it needs a paired test with more cohorts, not a verdict.
- "Why not [[f_cond_share_100]]%?": urgent booking for every referred patient gives [[f_cond_share_0]]% / [[f_cond_share_50]]% / [[f_cond_share_100]]% at 0 / 50 / 100% of the assumed lead reduction. It depends entirely on assumed urgent booking leads, so it is a conditional finding that needs real booking data, not a fast lane and not our headline.
- "How certain is [[f_share_base]]% to [[f_share_opt]]%?": the two held-out cohorts are the headline. Across [[r_f_cohorts]] cohorts today is [[r_f_base_lo]] to [[r_f_base_hi]]% and the suggested change [[r_f_sug_lo]] to [[r_f_sug_hi]]%, so quote the gain: [[r_f_gain_lo]] to [[r_f_gain_hi]] points, positive in every cohort. It nearly vanishes if CT capacity is one slot lower, and the capacities are our assumptions. Norway on simulated 100-patient cohorts at the best fit: [[s_obs_share]]% is [[r_n_obs_lo]] to [[r_n_obs_hi]], the authors' [[s_auth_share]]% is [[r_n_auth_lo]] to [[r_n_auth_hi]], the optimiser's [[s_opt_share]]% is [[r_n_opt_lo]] to [[r_n_opt_hi]] and [[s_noeq_share]]% is [[r_n_noeq_lo]] to [[r_n_noeq_hi]].
