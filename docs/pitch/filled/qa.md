# Q&A prep: the 23 hardest questions

Short answers, about 15 to 30 seconds each. Numbers are filled from the bundle and the robustness files (`filled/qa.md`); the single place to update a number is `numbers_sheet.md`. Questions 1 to 12 are the original set (wording corrected after the robustness check); 13 to 23 are the added hard ones (13 to 20 from the robustness check and the patient engine, 21 to 23 from the independent review `docs/review_w7.md`). Where an answer depends on a pending team decision (D17, D18, D20) it says so. Questions 24 and 25 were added after the optimiser robustness check.

**1. Isn't this just synthetic?**
Yes, all of it, and every results screen says so. Two things stand in for real data: every scenario input carries a source tag or is marked as an assumption, and the engine is checked against a published Norwegian pathway (the check gives a band, not a point; question 13). Be plain about the split: the Finnish scenario file has 49 input blocks (`docs/ledger.md`), 9 sourced and 40 assumptions, and the assumptions include every capacity and booking lead (calibrated to published waits); the Norwegian file has 25 input blocks, 3 assumptions. The larger parameter table is evidence and calibration targets, not all simulator inputs. The claim is "worth piloting", not "works in Finland".

**2. Why should Finland trust a Norwegian calibration?**
It does not have to. The Norwegian case tests the engine's reordering logic against one published result, with only the observed pathway fitted. Finnish numbers come from a separate scenario with Finnish sources and assumed capacities. One study, one centre, 100 patients, specialist care only.

**3. Does it match the Norwegian paper?**
It is consistent with it, not an exact match. Fitted: 40% against 40%, the only thing we fitted. Predicted: for the authors' pathway we get 80% at our best fit against their 80%, but across all 184 settings that fit the observed data the range is 62 to 86%, typically about 75%. We miss on the faster scanner alone: 60% against 48%. What holds everywhere is the ranking. The authors' 80% is itself a model estimate. Do not say "we predicted 80% exactly".

**4. How does it avoid hindsight bias?**
Proposals are scored on groups from revealed timestamps only, not by judging a late test on patients who failed earlier ones. Acceptance is a rerun of the same patients (common random numbers), and we report on held-out patients. Selection optimism: 1.7 points (Norwegian) and -0.2 (Finnish; negative means the unseen patients did slightly better). Did the Norwegian search "find" the authors' answer blind? See question 15.

**5. The acceptance step uses simulated truth. Isn't that cheating?**
It is the one step with no real-world equivalent, and we say so. In practice it is a monitored pilot. Proposals never see the hidden condition.

**6. Where is the AI?**
Three places: the optimiser (search over flowchart changes), the decision model (a transformer-style model that proposes the next step and is benchmarked against a dot-product baseline), and the AI-assisted build. The team's fine-tuned decision model (JEV) is connected to the decision nodes, but the runs we show use rules and placeholder decision tables (question 19). Our own small benchmark model, in our in-loop test, matched the hand-written rule rather than beating it, and it showed that a model optimising each patient separately overloads the shared queue unless it can see queue state. We would rather say that than overclaim.

**7. Does the decision model work?**
This is our own small benchmark model, not the team's JEV. On a task where the right answer depends on the option set, it scores 84.5% against 79.0% for a parameter-matched dot-product baseline (78.3% for the smaller d=32 one). On the control task it also wins by 2.5 points, so part of the gain is capacity. The "half the compute for a second question" claim did not reproduce: 76-91% at our sizes. Task and rule are ours; no public benchmark yet.

**8. What would a hospital do on Monday?**
Pick one lung unit. Reserve the treatment slot when the case is sent to the tumour board and book the specialist visit when the referral arrives, and measure days from first contact to treatment by step, including booking leads. The simulator says these two reorders take our county from 18% to 33% within 90 days with no new equipment. A bigger hand-written order-together pathway reaches 54% against 31% for the same extra capacity with today's order, but it needs PET-CT capacity, so check the scanner first.

**9. Why a 90-day target?**
Provisional (D20). From first contact, every flowchart is at 0% within the Norwegian 42 days, and we found no Finnish first-contact target. Median days (102 to 94) does not depend on the choice.

**10. Does the change widen inequality?**
Under our assumptions both groups gain (13% to 26% vague, 27% to 50% alarm). The gap in points widens (13.6 to 24.6); the gap in median days narrows (11.2 to 9.9). The group difference is our assumption (D9), so this shows how a change reaches each group, not how unequal Finland is.

**11. Does speed come at the cost of missed cancers or other patients?**
Not in the model: missed cancers 27.5 to 27.5 per cohort, tests per patient 0.41 to 0.41, non-cancer patients leave in 16.8 to 16.8 days. Guardrails include tissue-confirmed diagnoses, added after the optimiser tried skipping sampling; bronchoscopy and needle biopsy are marked as confirming in the Finnish scenario too, so that guardrail is active there. Crowd-out would depend on how many slots a priority track reserves; our accepted changes use none. (In the Norwegian check every patient has cancer, so the missed-cancer guardrail is vacuous there.)

**12. Isn't adding capacity simpler?**
Sometimes. One more slot at the busiest scanner (the PET-CT scanner) adds 9.7 points; the next best adds 3.6 and the rest at most 1.5. Hand-written reordering with extra capacity adds 36 points, and the optimiser's two reorders with no new capacity add 16 (against 13 for a large capacity increase with today's order). The two are not exclusive; the bigger reorder needs the capacity at the scanner it moves work to. Capacity vs order in our county is a result of our assumptions; the Norwegian study is the independent evidence.

## Added: validity, optimiser, data and deployment

**13. Why does the Norway validation say 62 to 86% and not 80%?**
Because the 80% is one point in a band. We fitted two numbers (the share within 42 days and the median) to set five inputs the paper does not report. Those two numbers carry nearly the same information, so many settings fit equally well. We first searched a narrow grid, where the best fit gave 80% and the range was 78 to 86%. When we widened the grid to 1920 points, 184 settings fit the observed pathway and the authors' pathway ranged from 62 to 86%, typically about 75%. About 64% of settings fall inside the paper's own interval (72-87%). So: consistent with the paper, ranking confirmed, exact size not pinned down. We found this ourselves and changed the headline. Source: `docs/validation.md`, "Robustness".

**14. You missed the faster-scanner prediction. Why trust the rest?**
We report the miss on the first slide that shows the check. At the best fit we give 60% against the published 48% (which was not statistically significant); across settings 50 to 74%, and only 34% of settings fall inside the published interval. We tried two fixes (re-discussion at the tumour board; delays that are correlated within a patient) and neither worked. Our reading, not proven: the model puts PET-CT on the critical path of more patients than the real hospital did. That means we probably overstate the value of speeding up one step, so the "order beats capacity" conclusion errs on the cautious side. We do not trust the magnitudes to better than about plus or minus 12 points; we trust the ranking, which holds in all 184 settings.

**15. Was the optimiser really blind?**
Blind to the hidden diagnosis: yes. Its proposals read only what steps revealed, and a test that scrambles the hidden fields after a run leaves its proposals unchanged. Blind to the answer: no. It chose from a menu of about 69 single changes that we wrote, and the menu contained each of the authors' changes as one edit. It was not told which ones the authors made. Starting from the observed pathway and without being told the answer, the optimiser arrived at the authors' redesign (their three changes, plus one overlapping change that needs a clinical check) and performs about the same as the authors' pathway built without a wait for sampling (88.3% against 88.2% at the best fit; within 0.2 to 2.5 points in every setting). It did not beat the authors. So the claim is that, given reasonable building blocks, the search rediscovers the authors' reordering; it is not discovery from nothing, and it is not an improvement on the authors. The optimiser's apparent lead over our simulated authors' pathway (88% against 80%) is not a finding about care: our version of the authors' pathway makes treatment wait for a tissue sample that starts after the tumour board, while the paper takes the sample while the patient waits for treatment; with that wait removed the authors' pathway gives 88%. We tested the optimiser's flowcharts on all 184 settings that fit the observed pathway: with the faster scanner 88% at the best fit; 78 to 93% across all 184 settings (median 88%); with no new equipment 67% at the best fit; 48 to 77% across all 184 settings (median 65%), against 36 to 44% for today's pathway. Without new equipment it beats today's pathway in 184 of 184 settings but beats the faster scanner alone in only 128 of 184; with the scanner it beats the scanner alone in 184 of 184; so "reordering beats buying the scanner" is only true in most settings, not all.

**16. What is the fourth change, and is it safe?**
It plans tissue sampling at the start, when the referral is triaged and the CT is booked, before a specialist has seen the patient. It was not in the paper. Without the fourth change the optimiser still gives 85% at the best fit with the scanner and 65% without; the fourth change is worth 2 to 4 points and overlaps with "tumour board before sampling". It was not in the paper and needs a clinical check. Removing it is a small effect: it is not where the optimiser's result comes from; the biggest single piece is ordering PET-CT early (about 13 points), which is one of the authors' own three. We have not had the fourth change reviewed by a clinician and we do not call it safe. Whether sampling before a specialist visit is appropriate depends on the case (for example the choice of procedure depends on tumour location). It is the first thing to put in front of a clinician. The guardrails the optimiser applies (no fewer tissue-confirmed diagnoses, no extra tests beyond a small tolerance) are met in the simulation (the missed-cancer check is vacuous there, because every Norwegian patient has cancer), which is not clinical approval.

**17. The patients are synthetic. Why would a model trained on them work on real patients?**
We do not know that it would, and we do not claim it. What we can say: the cards follow a catalogue of 11 conditions, 42 hidden variables and 26 tests, and an automatic audit passes. But 279 of 388 entries in the catalogue are tagged assumption, no clinician has read it, patient backgrounds come from a population model whose numbers are mostly assumptions, test results are independent of each other given the condition, disease does not progress while waiting, and only chest-symptom presenters are covered. A model trained here can be right about our synthetic world and wrong about real patients. This is what our review process is for: the audit passed, and the independent review still found a real leak in the report wording of model-written cards (50% of false-alarm reports hedge, against 2% of true positives). It is fixed in the code, and we report it rather than hide it: the new result prompt (g2@2: the engine sets every value, the language model writes report text from the reported values only) has not yet been run on the real model; the 2 530 model-written cards in the repository use the old prompt g2@1, and their report text is rebuilt from values before any decider sees it. What the synthetic data is good for: building and testing the machinery (the information boundary, the loop, the training export) before real data exists, and checking that a model does not just learn our template wording (train on template cards, test on model-written cards; this tests wording, not validity). The next step is clinician review and then real, permitted records.

**18. What can a decision-maker see, and what is hidden?**
It sees the visible record: background (age, sex, smoking, region), what the patient reported, and the result of each test that has been done, as positive or negative. It never sees the hidden condition, the hidden findings, which results are planned misses or false alarms, or the card's identity. A result appears only when that test is ordered. The record has two layers: a mechanical log written by the engine, and clinical assessments written by the decider. The training export keeps the hidden labels outside the input. The code paths were traced one by one by an independent review: the decision tables, the stored record and the team's model request contain no hidden field. The review found one leak, in model-written report text (wording that hedged on false alarms); it reached requests that carry report text and the training export. It is fixed: reports are written from reported values only (new prompt) or rebuilt from values before any decider sees them (old cards), and the audit now checks report wording. We do not claim the boundary is proven leak-free, and an end-to-end review of the boundary is the thing to ask for next.

**19. Are the decisions in your runs made by the JEV?**
No. The runs we show use a rule policy and placeholder decision tables, and every behaviour number in them is an assumption. The team's fine-tuned model (JEV) is connected through an adapter and a step-through tool, so you can walk a patient and see what it proposes. Its scores are relative to the offered options, not disease probabilities. Nothing we report depends on it. Also, our primary-care loop uses placeholder tables, and it reproduces the calibrated chain at the edge of the tolerances: 8 of 8 checks on the seeds it was tuned on, 6 of 8 on fresh seeds, 7 of 8 pooled over eight seeds; the numbers come from our assumptions, so we call it a mechanism, not a Finnish estimate. In it a cancer waits about 25 days to referral (the chain: 27); about one in ten (4 to 13%) is first treated as something else and those wait about 4 times as long (wide interval); no group difference is established.

**20. What would it take to use this in a Finnish wellbeing services county?**
Real data and clinical ownership, in this order. (1) The county's own pathway timestamps (referral, first visit, imaging, tumour board, treatment) to replace our assumed capacities and booking times; these are the assumptions the Finnish numbers lean on most. (2) A permit and data-protection route for using the records (secondary use of health data in Finland goes through Findata; we have not started this). (3) Calibrate on one period and test on another before showing any number. (4) A clinician review of the catalogue and of every proposed change, the fourth Norwegian one first. (5) A monitored pilot in one county with before-and-after measurement. (6) A check of whether any individual-patient use counts as medical-device software; we have not assessed this. Until then our claim is "worth piloting", not "works in Finland".

**21. Does faster care save lives in lung cancer?**
Not shown here. Across cancers, a four-week treatment delay is associated with higher mortality (Hanna et al., BMJ 2020), but for lung surgery the estimate (hazard ratio about 1.06) was not statistically significant, and a 2025 lung-specific meta-analysis is reported as null (`docs/evidence_research.md`). We use Finland's weak survival as context for the gap, and argue for shorter waits on their own terms. We do not claim lives saved.

**22. Finnish diagnosis-to-treatment is 37 days in your model. Lung-specific data say about 69.**
Correct, and unresolved. The 37 days (IHE, 2018-2022) is not lung-specific; a 2018 study in five Finnish hospitals gives a median of 69 days for surgical patients. We have not rerun the Finnish shares with the longer value, so the Finnish levels may be optimistic on that step. The claim that two reorders help in every cohort does not depend on it, but the size might.

**23. The change widens the gap between symptom groups. Is that equal care?**
In percentage points the gap widens, from 13.6 to 24.6, because the alarm-symptom group starts closer to the threshold; both groups gain, and the gap in median days narrows. We report both and do not claim the gap closes. The group difference is our assumption (D9). The hand-written priority track for vague symptoms is the option that most narrows the gap in points, with a crowd-out guardrail and real booking data needed first.

**24. The optimiser scores higher than the authors' pathway. Did it improve on the paper?**
No, and we do not say so. The optimiser's apparent lead over our simulated authors' pathway (88% against 80%) is not a finding about care: our version of the authors' pathway makes treatment wait for a tissue sample that starts after the tumour board, while the paper takes the sample while the patient waits for treatment; with that wait removed the authors' pathway gives 88%. Fix that one difference and the two are within 0.2 to 2.5 points of each other in every setting. What we claim is the rediscovery: from the observed pathway alone the optimiser arrived at the authors' three changes plus one overlapping change that needs a clinical check.

**25. Does your Norway version of the authors' pathway follow the paper?**
Partly. Our simulated authors' pathway differs from the paper's text in two ways that offset each other: treatment waits for the tissue sample (lowers ours by about 13.5 points) and PET-CT is ordered at triage instead of the first visit (raises ours by about 14.1). A stricter reading of the paper's text (PET-CT at the first visit, treatment not waiting for sampling) gives a median of 79% over the 184 settings (64 to 90%; 139 of 184 inside the published 72-87%) and 77% at the best fit. Caveats: we read summaries of the paper, not its figures, and the observed pathway was not recalibrated under that reading; the shipped scenario is unchanged and this is an open decision for the human lead. The headline stays: consistent with the published 80%, range 62 to 86%.

## If pressed

Without the fourth change the optimiser still gives 85% at the best fit with the scanner and 65% without; the fourth change is worth 2 to 4 points and overlaps with "tumour board before sampling". It was not in the paper and needs a clinical check. With the faster scanner: 88% at the best fit; 78 to 93% across all 184 settings (median 88%); without new equipment 67% at the best fit; 48 to 77% across all 184 settings (median 65%), against 36 to 44% for today's pathway (D17 stays open for Tuomas; the demo pages show the like-for-like pair).
- GP-first instead of nurse-first was not accepted: it did not pass our safety checks on the training patients (half a missed cancer per cohort more, one patient in one cohort), while on unseen patients it looked neutral to positive. That is noise-level either way, so it needs a paired test with more cohorts, not a verdict.
- "Why not 61%?": urgent booking for every referred patient gives 18% / 44% / 61% at 0 / 50 / 100% of the assumed lead reduction. It depends entirely on assumed urgent booking leads, so it is a conditional finding that needs real booking data, not a fast lane and not our headline.
- "How certain is 18% to 33%?": the two held-out cohorts are the headline. Across 12 cohorts today is 14 to 25% and the suggested change 24 to 42%, so quote the gain: 9 to 19 points, positive in every cohort. It nearly vanishes if CT capacity is one slot lower, and the capacities are our assumptions. Norway on simulated 100-patient cohorts at the best fit: 40% is 30 to 51, the authors' 80% is 72 to 87, the optimiser's 88% is 81 to 94 and 67% is 56 to 75.
