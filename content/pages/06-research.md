# Research: where the time goes

Finland's national cancer strategy for 2026 to 2035 sets a clear goal: equal and effective care, with patients reaching treatment within target times. Lung cancer is where Finland falls furthest behind its Nordic neighbours, both in how quickly cancer is diagnosed and in how many patients survive.

## The core problem is waiting

The delay starts early. In Finland, 40 to 60 percent of patients with lung symptoms first see a nurse, while in the other Nordic countries they see a doctor. Fewer than 10 percent of Finnish primary care units can order a CT scan directly. At each step of the lung cancer pathway Finland is slower: about 14 days to a health centre appointment against 2 to 7 days elsewhere in the Nordics, 14 to 28 days to a specialist against 7 to 14, and about 28 days from specialist to diagnosis.

Where the delay builds up also varies. In one study from Satakunta, the referral took about two weeks, but the diagnosis took almost two more months, so most of the delay was in specialist care. Time is lost mostly in queues between steps, not in the tests themselves. A queue forms wherever patients arrive faster than a step can serve them, and a late or out-of-order referral makes it worse.

## Capacity or order?

The obvious answer to long queues is more capacity: another scanner, more staff. The evidence suggests that the order of steps often matters more. A Norwegian study of 100 early-stage lung cancer patients found that only 40 percent started treatment within the 42-day target. A new PET-CT scanner that cut the scan wait from about three weeks to one would have raised that to 48 percent, a gain that was not statistically significant. Reordering the steps, so that scans are ordered at the first visit and the tumour board meets straight after them, would have raised it to 80 percent.

Other cases point the same way. An English hospital cut the wait for a CT after an abnormal X-ray from 15 days to 3, yet the time to a treatment decision did not change, because the later steps were untouched. Where whole pathways were redesigned, times fell: in Toronto, ordering tests before the first visit cut referral to diagnosis from about 40 to 28 days, and London's Royal Free cut referral to treatment from 66 to 29 days.

Reordering has limits. Some tests are genuinely undersized, and no reordering fixes that. Fast tracks can also make other patients wait longer. This is why our simulator reports waits for every patient, not only those with cancer, and why it points out the bottlenecks where extra capacity would actually help.
